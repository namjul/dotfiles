## Level Analysis

Rubric rows read across the six OntoEdit levels. Level 1 is the shallowest ontology (discrete things, local causes); Level 6 the deepest (active field, phase states, evaluative gradients).

### Primitives

#### Global

- **Level 1:** Uninvolved, Local Causes. Local objects only. World described as discrete things/events; "context" is backdrop. No system boundary or state notion.
- **Level 2:** Background, arena, stage, inconsequential, passive "container", may cite background features to "set the stage," subjects move forward, backward, up, down "in the arena" not "with it."
- **Level 3:** Containing, constraining, but passively, like obstacles or boundaries. Environment as constraints. World= external conditions shaping outcomes ("constraints," "inputs," "stimuli". State is mostly outside the agent; passive setting
- **Level 4:** Influences are consequential, relationally networked, but de-animated, still passive, background may be composed of subsystems that are relationally networked. System state (but modular). Recognizes interacting parts; uses "system," "feedback," "networks." Still tends to treat subsystems as separable; boundaries assumed not argued.
- **Level 5:** Active co-creator in self-organizing dynamics, affordances and temporal flows, agent-arena boundary porous. State as coupled dynamics. State is a trajectory; agent <>world co-determination, history dependent. Tracks how changing conditions change what becomes possible.
- **Level 6:** Active organizing principle, dynamic phase states, evaluative gradients, metaphysical primitives for individuation, threshold events give rise to agency-without-agents. State is an active possibility with basins/thresholds/paths. Explicitly treats "the situation" as a structured field that produces intelligible moves/events

#### State/Potential

- **Level 1:** Potential is fixed by immutable laws/destiny/biological or physical constraints due to types
- **Level 2:** Potential is built up or constructed by effort and practice, stage-like
- **Level 3:** Potential is a developmental, adjacent possible, evolutionary and emergent.
- **Level 4:** Potential is afforded by networks, network relations at scale, increasingly complicated/complexified.
- **Level 5:** Potential is relational, co-created schema between agents, self-organized systems
- **Level 6:** Potential is unexplored, unexploited relations latent in the field; sometimes intuited/recognized but not fully understood.

#### Naturalized

- **Level 1:** Physical materialism reserved for objective world only, exclusive subject-object dualism. Intuitive folk explanation. Mentalistic or narrative causation, little operational discipline
- **Level 2:** Behavior and culture naturalized by assigning natural laws, population dynamics. Loose empirical anchoring. Mentions data or biology but mechanisms are vague.
- **Level 3:** Subjectivity is naturalized but through reductive physicalism like brain-behavior correlates. Mechanistic framing (partial). Uses identifiable mechanisms, but leaps from correlation to explanation.
- **Level 4:** Subjectivity is transjective distributed collectively among other subjects, embedded in cultural milieu. Operational mechanism. Clear constructs + measurement logic: distinguishes correlation vs causation; knows what intervention would test.
- **Level 5:** Subjectivity is enacted through exchange dynamics with biological and ecological "subjects." Naturalized across scales. Links mechanism across levels (cell-circuit-behavior-context) without collapsing them. Shows what explanatory job belongs to which stratum.
- **Level 6:** Non-local agents, agency is in what the field is made with, local and global dynamics and flows, manifolds, topological "spaces."

#### Evaluative

- **Level 1:** Facts only, values occluded or denied.       Absent or treated as noise. Values/motivation/meaning excluded or dismissed as “subjective.”
- **Level 2:** Values as local incentives preceding behaviors, no reflexivity, Values named,  not structural.  Mentions reward, fear, preference, ethics—but as add-ons
- **Level 3:** Values as verbs- evaluation, evaluating, mostly cost-benefit analysis type Evaluation as functional variable Evaluation appears as a measurable driver (reward prediction, salience, cost). Still framed as a variable inside a model, not as a background field. Reward affects choices.
- **Level 4:** Evaluative dynamics as powerful drivers of collective behavior, “we create our systems and afterwards they create us” – evaluative systems can de-animate their members, uni-directional influence.
- **Level 5:** Evaluative gradients shape dynamics. Shows how value/salience alters thresholds, learning, trajectories. Evaluation is not just content; it is shaping.
- **Level 6:** Evaluative manifold as generative ground. Evaluation defines what counts as signal/noise, option/affordance, agent/world boundary. Explicit about “what makes something matter” in the system. evaluation constitutes the space of possible moves

## PS Rubric Module

OntoEdit PS Rubric Module — Five Standards Across Levels (L1–L6)

**Monotonic increase rule (PS).** From L1 → L6, capacity must be non-decreasing on each PS standard: Precision, Prediction, Causality, Mechanism, Equivalences. Any upgrade that trades off a standard must name the loss, specify boundary conditions, and provide a falsification / contact test.

**How to use.** Use the table to characterize what each standard can support at each OntoEdit level. Higher levels should show wider regime validity, stronger counterfactual control, tighter constraints, and better transport across contexts via invariants (where applicable).

### Precision

- **L1:** Calibrated measurement with units, uncertainty, and protocol; reliable description in the observed setup.
- **L2:** Operational definitions survive simple manipulations; repeatable across nearby conditions; reduced ambiguity.
- **L3:** Decomposed by components/variables; improved identifiability and sensitivity (what to measure, where, when).
- **L4:** Regime-aware: phase boundaries, context variables, and measurement failure modes.
- **L5:** Objective-relevant state: tighter error budgets where it matters for stability/selection/control.
- **L6:** Invariant/coordinate-robust: dimensionless groups, conserved quantities, symmetry-aware measurement (where applicable).

### Prediction

- **L1:** Empirical prediction within a narrow envelope; interpolation/extrapolation without intervention semantics.
- **L2:** Intervention-conditional: change X by Δ → expected Y with quantified range.
- **L3:** Mechanism-linked: perturbations/ablations produce diagnostic signatures.
- **L4:** Regime-conditional: same intervention yields different outcomes across contexts; predicts which regime you’re in.
- **L5:** Trajectory-level: predicts stabilization/selection/optimization outcomes under constraints (what the system settles into).
- **L6:** Transportable: generalizes across transformations (scale/boundary/coordinates) via invariants.

### Causality

- **L1:** Causal talk mostly tracks correlation + temporal ordering; weak identification.
- **L2:** Manipulation-based: controlled changes establish direct effects; basic confound handling begins.
- **L3:** Pathways: mediated structure, direct vs indirect effects; targeted intervention map.
- **L4:** Contextual: causal relations shift with boundary conditions/regimes; identifies where arrows flip/disappear.
- **L5:** Constraint/selection causality: includes stabilization principles and why trajectories persist under constraints.
- **L6:** Manifold causality: systematic counterfactual navigation; equivalence classes of interventions; controllability/observability logic (where relevant).

### Mechanism

- **L1:** Phenomenology / black-box functional description (“it behaves like…”).
- **L2:** Minimal generative story with at least one discriminating test; narrows plausible process classes.
- **L3:** Parts + interactions: breakable model; perturbations/ablations predict specific signatures.
- **L4:** Multi-scale + boundary coupling: includes environment/constraints; specifies regime failure modes.
- **L5:** Stabilization: adds feedback/constraint satisfaction/variational or selection dynamics explaining recurrence.
- **L6:** Designable families: compositional modularity; principled edits that make new outcomes reachable and repeatable.

### Equivalences

- **L1:** Basic algebraic relations, curve fits, unit-consistent empirical regularities.
- **L2:** Scaling relations / dimensional analysis that survive interventions within a regime.
- **L3:** Model equations tied to mechanism (rates, conservation, constitutive relations).
- **L4:** Regime-bridging formalisms: dimensionless numbers, bifurcations, effective theories; what changes vs persists.
- **L5:** Principle-level equivalences: variational/optimization-under-constraints; multiple descriptions shown mathematically identical.
- **L6:** Deep invariants/symmetries: coordinate-free statements; group/gauge structure (where relevant); mappings that transfer results across domains.

## Interpretive Schema Ladder

The Interpretive Schema Ladder evaluates the strength of a proposed relation between two domains, concepts, phenomena, or explanatory structures.

It prevents weak comparisons from being overclaimed. A metaphor is not a homology. A useful analogy is not yet an isomorphism. The ladder asks: What kind of interpretive relation is actually being asserted?

### L1 - Metaphor

A metaphor carries imaginative, heuristic, or perceptual value. It helps the reader see one domain through another, but it does not yet claim functional, structural, or dynamic correspondence.
*Example:* “The organism is a river.”
This may illuminate flow, continuity, or change, but it does not yet specify shared mechanisms.
Merit: opens perception.
Risk: poetic inflation; treating suggestive likeness as explanatory proof.

### L2 - Functional Analogy

A functional analogy identifies a shared role or operation across domains, even if the underlying structures differ.
*Example:* “The immune system and a legal system both distinguish tolerated from intolerable events.”
The analogy is stronger than metaphor because it identifies a comparable function, but it does not yet prove common structure.
Merit: supports cross-domain learning.
Risk: function drift; ignoring differences in substrate, scale, and causality.

### L3 - Structural Homology

A structural homology claims that two domains share a deeper relational architecture. The parts may differ materially, but the organization of relations is significantly parallel.
*Example:* “Bioelectric morphogenesis and institutional governance both involve distributed threshold-regulation across an evaluative field.”
Here the claim is not merely that they “do something similar,” but that their relational architecture can be mapped.
Merit: enables real comparative theory.
Risk: false homology; importing structure where only surface resemblance exists.

### L4 - Dynamic Isomorphism

A dynamic isomorphism claims that two systems share a transformation pattern, not merely a structure. Their processes evolve according to comparable dynamics across time, thresholds, feedback loops, phase changes, or attractor transitions.
*Example:* “Embryonic repair, collective learning, and institutional reform all involve a system re-entering possibility space after perturbation, searching for a new stable schema through distributed evaluative correction.”
This is the strongest interpretive claim. It requires contact tests, not merely elegance.
Merit: supports serious transdisciplinary science/metatheory.
Risk: totalizing overreach; claiming universal dynamics without empirical or operational anchors.

## SS Level Bands

### SS L3 — Newtonian humanism / control-pipeline realism. World is legible “out there”; linear causation; power as resources→conversion→outcomes; risk dominates.

- **Markers / Indicators:** Decomposable actors; stationarity assumed; uncertainty reduced to missing info; revealed preferences; optimizing rationality; equilibrium/gaussian assumptions.
- **Typical tools:** Linear causal models; equilibrium reasoning; regression/pipeline logic; hard/soft power conversion frameworks.
- **Failure modes:** Small-world imperialism; risk=uncertainty subtracted; false universality; overconfidence in Bayes-as-obligation.
- **Guardrails:** Distinguish risk vs uncertainty; disclose stationarity; avoid ‘true probability’ rhetoric for large worlds.
- **Upgrade move:** Add context as active; acknowledge irreducible uncertainty and boundary conditions; move toward map/territory discipline.

### SS L3–4 — Quantified complexity without ontology shift. More knobs, same worldview.

- **Markers / Indicators:** ‘Just enrich the model’; add covariates; better priors; path dependence as extra terms; large world assumed to add up from small worlds.
- **Typical tools:** Bigger regressions; sensitivity analyses; Bayesian updating as universal nomological machine.
- **Failure modes:** Made-of / additivity fallacy; technique substitutes for ontology; late-stage L4 trap precursor.
- **Guardrails:** Ask what cannot be aggregated; test for regime change and category failure.
- **Upgrade move:** Separate epistemology from ontology; adopt explicit map≠territory warnings.

### SS L4 — Map–territory discipline + paradigm awareness. Context matters; simplification is a useful capitulation.

- **Markers / Indicators:** Explicit humility; model limits; heterogeneous causalities; path dependence; interpretation acknowledged but often descriptive.
- **Typical tools:** Boundary conditions; robustness & scope conditions; plural models; qualitative contextualization.
- **Failure modes:** Context-only flatland; endless caveats (late L4 stall) without redesigning method.
- **Guardrails:** Require operational handles and contact tests; don’t stop at warnings.
- **Upgrade move:** Move to risk–uncertainty complementarity; treat uncertainty as irreducible and method-shaping.

### SS L4–5 — Large-world entry + pragmatist evaluation. Risk and uncertainty complementary; rationalities plural; preferences constructed.

- **Markers / Indicators:** Fat tails/black swans; epistemic vs ontological uncertainty; constructed preferences; framing effects; experimentation/learning-by-doing.
- **Typical tools:** Scenario work; pragmatic model evaluation (richtig vs wahr); iterative intervention; nonstationarity awareness.
- **Failure modes:** Usefulness criteria left implicit; language treated as secondary; uncertainty re-domesticated as risk.
- **Guardrails:** Make evaluation criteria explicit; keep world pushback visible.
- **Upgrade move:** Treat models/language as performative (engines not cameras).

### SS L5 — Performativity / world-making. Language, metaphor, and models have effects; potentialities and uncertainty are structural.

- **Markers / Indicators:** Narrative turn; models as engines; retroactive sensemaking; anti-nomological-machine; protean power; meaning arrives late.
- **Typical tools:** Representation ecology analysis; performativity tracing; metaphor diagnostics; interpretive mechanisms.
- **Failure modes:** Pseudo-L5 relativism (‘trapped in language’); loss of contact with constraints.
- **Guardrails:** Mechanism + friction + reanimation clauses; treat language as public normative field; include resistance and consequences.
- **Upgrade move:** Cross-scale consilience; deep time; more-than-human agency; explicit anchor & drift.

### SS L5–6 — Consilience + multi-scale entanglement (often evaluative operator still implicit). Deep time, more-than-human, cross-domain synthesis.

## Yellow Flags

A yellow flag identifies a problematic thought-form that may weaken, distort, or inflate an argument.

A yellow flag does not automatically make the argument fail. It marks a risk requiring clarification, contact testing, or re-scaling.

A red flag occurs when one or more problematic thought-forms has become structurally operative inside the argument.

At that point, the argument fails, not because it contains an imperfection, but because the error-form is doing necessary argumentative work.

### Y1 - Slogan Thinking

A canned maxim (“the whole is greater than the sum of its parts,” “different parts of the same elephant,” a familiar “both/and” synthesis) is repeated until it feels profound. Social warmth and recognizability stand in for argument.

*Diagnostic question:* If the slogan were removed, would any argument remain?

### Y2 - Decorative Technicality

Mathematics, physics, Gödel, topology, quantum or Whiteheadian vocabulary is borrowed for its depth-effect rather than for the constraints, distinctions, or theorems it actually carries. A technical concept is legitimate only if it returns with real constraint.

*Diagnostic question:* What constraint or theorem does the technical term bring back that the plain claim lacked?

### Y3 - Lexical Inflation

An ordinary event is renamed in ornate vocabulary (“concrescence” for “something happened”) and multisyllabic language is mistaken for depth.

*Diagnostic question:* Restated in plain words, is the claim still interesting?

### Y4 - Illicit Ontological Inference

“X is not fundamental, therefore X is not real”; “X cannot be defined in simpler terms, therefore X is fundamental.” Failures of reduction, definition, or formal capture show a gap, not a settled metaphysical conclusion.

*Diagnostic question:* Has an explanatory gap been converted into an ontological verdict?

### Y5 - Sense Conflation

One word, several problems. “Irreducibility” alone can mean compositional, reflexive, or conceptual irreducibility, and the argument slides between them as though they were one. Much philosophy lives off such hidden equivocations.

*Diagnostic question:* Which sense is in play at each step, and does the conclusion survive if the senses are held apart?

### Y6 - Local-to-Global Overreach

The reverse elephant: agreement among local reports is taken to settle the global structure, when the whole may still be nontrivial, blocked, or extensible in more than one way. The move fails for easy holism, easy reductionism, and easy synthesis alike.

*Diagnostic question:* Do the local patches actually glue, and is there more than one way to glue them?

### Y7 - Trivial Joints

Levels are chained (psychology to neurology to biology to chemistry to physics) as if a sequence of local dependencies automatically yields a global explanation. The cross-domain seams are exactly where the stitching may fail.

*Diagnostic question:* At each joint, what has been shown rather than assumed?

### Y8 - Unity Fetish

Unification is treated as intrinsically superior to distinction, so disunity, conceptual splitting, entity-resolution, and non-equivalence go unexplored. What looks like one thing may be several, and local agreement may hide global incoherence.

*Diagnostic question:* Would this be better understood as two things rather than one?

### Y9 - Deflationary “Merely”

“Consciousness is merely…,” “meaning is merely…,” “agency is merely…” The word lets a problem be redescribed, diminished, or displaced while appearing solved.

*Diagnostic question:* Has the problem been solved, or relocated?

### Y10 - Concept Stripping

A thick concept (understanding, character, information, agency, intelligence, sentience, prediction) is cut down to a thin operational proxy, extended to machines or simple systems, and the loss goes unacknowledged. The original is flattened and the new case inflated.

*Diagnostic question:* What did the thick concept carry that the proxy drops, and is the extension trading on the dropped part?

### Y11 - Formalization Idolatry

A definition or model built to serve an implicit phenomenon becomes the standard, and the phenomenon is declared unreal or confused when it resists the model: formula-to-ontology, syntax-to-semantics, failure of formal capture taken as proof of nonexistence.

*Diagnostic question:* Which is being corrected against which: the model against the phenomenon, or the phenomenon against the model?

### Y12 - Smuggled Presuppositions

An apparently methodological demand hides a metaphysical one: requiring that consciousness be defined in non-conscious terms, or labeling counterexamples “unphysical” only because they threaten the preferred conclusion. The solution is by fiat.

*Diagnostic question:* What is the argument demanding be granted before it starts?

## Conjunctive Arguments

A conjunctive argument gains strength when the same claim, structure, or explanatory pattern holds across multiple independent domains, registers, or OntoEdit levels.

It is not merely interdisciplinary ornament. It is a bridge-test.

A claim becomes stronger when it can be translated downward into operational handles and upward into a more general metaphysical or theoretical frame without losing its core structure.

### Type 1 - Cross Domain Arguments

The argument holds across distinct domains.
*Example:* A claim about evaluative co-variance holds in embryogenesis, plant intelligence, machine agency, and institutional governance.
Merit: supports generality.
Risk: superficial universalism.

### Type 2 - Cross-Level Conjunction

The argument holds across OntoEdit levels.
*Example:* A claim works at L3 as an empirical mechanism, at L4 as a systems model, at L5 as a reflexive/corrigible frame, and at L6 as a metaphysical principle.
Merit: shows vertical coherence.
Risk: level confusion.

### Type 3 - Philosophy-Science Bridge

The argument can be stated both philosophically and scientifically without either version collapsing the other.
*Example:* Philosophically: agency is not separable from reality.
Scientifically: agency emerges through organism-environment co-variance, threshold dynamics, and schema formation.
Merit: bridges meaning and mechanism.
Risk: pseudo-consilience.

### Type 4 - Interpretive-Operational Bridge

The argument begins as an interpretive claim but can be translated into empirical, institutional, design, or behavioral tests.
*Example:* “Plants predicate worlds” becomes testable through niche construction, growth steering, memory, signaling, and environment-modifying behavior.
Merit: prevents metaphysical rhetoric from floating free.
Risk: reductive operationalization.

### Type 5 - Upward-Downward Translation

The argument can move upward into abstraction and downward into concrete contact.
*Example:* “Evaluative fields” can be stated metaphysically, biologically, cognitively, and practically without losing its function.
Merit: strong OntoEdit performance.
Risk: translation loss or false equivalence.
